\subsection{Abstract}\label{abstract}

Large language models (LLMs) fail silently at long-horizon generation. A
sword picked up in chapter 8 is forgotten by chapter 20; a character's
death goes unreported; and the model itself cannot say what it believes
the world looks like. Existing benchmarks for long-form generation
measure surface quality---fluency, coherence, consistency-error
density---but stop short of the question that matters for downstream
agents: \emph{when a system is required to maintain a queryable world
state alongside the text, is that state consistent with what it wrote,
and can every claim be traced to evidence?}

We introduce \textbf{ShadowBench-W}, a benchmark for state consistency
in long-form story continuation. Built on a Chinese corpus in two
scales---an S-level 10-chapter (\textasciitilde20K characters)
development corpus and an \textbf{M-level 50-chapter (≈95K characters)
main corpus}---each with a 5-chapter continuation task, it defines two
scores: \textbf{W1} measures consistency errors per 100 contacts (EPC)
in the generated text, and \textbf{W3} measures field-level agreement
between the system's claimed world state and ground truth, plus two
separately reported evidence numbers --- \emph{coverage} (of the fields
under test, how many carry source-traceable evidence) and
\emph{precision} (of the evidence supplied, how much is traceable). We
also present the \textbf{Origin IR state layer}, a reference method that
compiles context under a token budget and validates every state mutation
as a transaction carrying an evidence chain.

On the \textbf{M-level} task (50-chapter, ≈95K-character baseline, 10
rounds/arm, two unrelated models), bare models score 56.3\% / 53.8\% on
W3 and vector RAG does not fix it (70.0\% / 58.8\%). A full state layer
reaches 100.0\% / 97.5\%. \textbf{But an ablation shows the machinery is
not what produces this.} A prompt-only arm --- the model is simply told
to emit and update an explicit world state each chapter, with no
validator, no evidence chain, and no context compiler --- reaches
\textbf{97.5\% / 95.0\%}, capturing 41.3 of the state layer's 43.8-point
advantage on \emph{both} models; the residual 2.5 points are not
significant (p = 0.47 / 0.72). The prompt-only arm is also
\textbf{cheaper than the bare baseline} (−9.7\% / −28.2\% tokens), while
the full state layer costs +13.7\% / +21.1\%.

The premise survives a frontier control: a flagship model (qwen3.7-max)
writes the best prose we have measured (EPC 0.60) and still scores
\textbf{56.3\%} on state --- state corruption is a missing mechanism,
not a capability gap that scale closes.

What the machinery does buy is the thing prompting cannot produce at
all: an evidence chain. Prompt-only and bare arms have zero evidence
coverage by construction; the state layer attaches source-traceable
evidence to \textbf{27.5\% / 15.0\%} of the fields under test --- a real
categorical difference, and far below the ``100\%'' we reported in v0.3
under a self-selected denominator. Our headline finding is therefore not
\emph{``build a state layer''} but \emph{``ask for the state''}: the
expensive part of the system reproduces almost none of the benefit its
own authors attributed to it, and ShadowBench-W is what made that
visible. The benchmark, judges, all four arms, and 90 per-round result
files are released under Apache-2.0.

\subsection{1. Introduction}\label{introduction}

\textbf{Motivating scenario.} Ask an LLM to continue a novel for 50
chapters, then ask it: \emph{whose hand is the protagonist's sword in
now?} A baseline model cannot answer---not because it failed to read the
earlier chapters, but because it was \emph{never required to maintain} a
queryable world state. While generating, it wrote ``the sword is in Zhao
Qi's hands'' into some passage, but the next generation step may freely
write ``Lin Zheng draws his sword''---the text contradicts itself and
the model does not notice, because no mechanism aligns \emph{what it
wrote} with \emph{what it believes}.

We call this defect \textbf{state corruption}: generated content reads
fluently at a local level yet contradicts itself over long horizons, and
the system can neither detect, localize, nor explain its own errors.

\textbf{Three structural defects.} Failures in long-form generation can
be attributed to three families (full argument in MANIFESTO.md):

\begin{enumerate}
\def\labelenumi{\arabic{enumi}.}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{Perception}: the model never systematically reads the earlier
  context---the context window cannot hold 50 chapters, and tail
  truncation drops critical information.
\item
  \textbf{Memory}: the model has no persistent state---``the context
  always overflows'' is a memory defect, not a length problem.
\item
  \textbf{Output validation}: the model cannot verify what it
  writes---there is no commit gate, no rollback, no evidence chain.
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{The blind spot of existing benchmarks.} Long-context benchmarks
(LongBench et al.) ask ``can the model \emph{use} a long context to
answer questions''. Story-continuation benchmarks measure text quality
and even intra-text contradiction (ConStory-Bench, arXiv 2603.05890).
But ``when a system is \emph{required} to maintain a queryable world
state, is that state consistent with the text, and is every field
traceable to evidence''---no public benchmark covers this.
ConStory-Bench detects contradictions \emph{in prose after the fact};
ShadowBench-W evaluates the \emph{state-writeback contract}: can the
system say what it believes, and justify it? The distinction matters for
any agent that must act on state (writing, coding, operations): it needs
to answer ``what do I believe now, and why''.

\textbf{Contributions.} We contribute four things:

\begin{enumerate}
\def\labelenumi{\arabic{enumi}.}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{A new task definition}: the state-writeback contract in
  long-form continuation---requiring the system to maintain a queryable,
  verifiable world state \emph{as a first-class object}, and evaluating
  whether that state agrees with the text and can be justified
  field-by-field. Distinct from intra-text contradiction detection
  (ConStory-Bench).
\item
  \textbf{A new benchmark, ShadowBench-W}: a two-scale Chinese corpus
  (S-level ≈20K, M-level ≈95K characters), 5-chapter continuation tasks,
  and two judges. W1 measures consistency errors per contact (EPC); W3
  measures state-writeback correctness --- field-level accuracy,
  evidence \textbf{coverage}, and evidence \textbf{precision}, reported
  separately (§3.2). Its first serious use falsified the method its own
  authors proposed (§5.3), which we regard as the strongest available
  evidence that it measures something.
\item
  \textbf{A negative result about our own method}: the Origin IR state
  layer (context-compiler + commit-compiler, transactional state with
  evidence chains) is \emph{not} what produces the accuracy gain. A
  prompt-only arm reaches within 2.5 points of it on both models (p =
  0.48 / 0.72) at lower token cost than even the bare baseline. We
  report this because the benchmark we built is what detected it; a
  benchmark that cannot embarrass its author is not measuring anything.
\item
  \textbf{A separation of two things the field conflates}: \emph{state
  accuracy} (``can the system say what it believes'') is obtainable by
  prompting alone; \emph{evidence traceability} (``can it say why'') is
  not obtainable by prompting at all, and remains largely unsolved even
  with machinery --- our own coverage is 27.5\% / 15.0\%. Systems that
  must justify their beliefs, not merely state them, are where a state
  layer still has something to prove.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{2. Related Work}\label{related-work}

\begin{quote}
Citations below were verified live on 2026-08-07 (arXiv IDs confirmed to
exist with matching titles; star counts reported approximate). Re-verify
once more before final submission, per the repository rule ``citations
before claims''.
\end{quote}

\textbf{Long-context evaluation.} LongBench (arXiv 2308.14508),
LongBench v2 (arXiv 2412.15204), and ∞Bench (arXiv 2402.13718) measure
whether a model can \emph{use} long context for question answering; they
do not measure consistency of \emph{generation} over long horizons.

\textbf{Story generation and continuation.} ConStory-Bench (arXiv
2603.05890, ``Lost in Stories: Consistency Bugs in Long Story Generation
by LLMs'') is the closest and most important prior art. It evaluates
narrative consistency with 2,000 prompts across four task scenarios,
defines a taxonomy of five error categories with 19 fine-grained
subtypes, and builds ConStory-Checker, an automated pipeline that
detects contradictions and grounds each judgment in explicit textual
evidence. It finds consistency errors are most common in factual and
temporal dimensions, cluster around the middle of narratives, and
correlate with high-entropy text segments.

ConStory-Bench and ShadowBench-W measure \emph{different things}.
ConStory-Bench detects \textbf{intra-text contradictions} in
already-generated prose (text vs.~text). ShadowBench-W's W3 measures
\textbf{state-writeback correctness}: when the system is \emph{required
to maintain} a queryable world state (a first-class object, not an
artifact of the text), does that state agree with the generated text
field-by-field, and can every field be traced to evidence? The two are
complementary: ConStory-Bench asks ``did the story contradict itself?'',
ShadowBench-W asks ``can the system \emph{say} what it believes, and
justify it?''---the question that matters for downstream agents acting
on the state. Evidence traceability (W3's second score) has no
counterpart in ConStory-Bench.

Tianming (zy-zmc/tianming-novel-ai-writer) is a production writing
assistant with 15-dimensional fact snapshots and 12 types of CHANGES
declarations---closest in spirit on the ``maintain state while writing''
side, but it does not publish a benchmark protocol or open judges.

\textbf{World-model representations and transactional memory.} OpenUSD
(PixarAnimationStudios/OpenUSD, \textasciitilde7.4k★, active) has made
``save the source, project on demand'' an industry standard in 3D.
Origin IR applies the same idea to textual world state. Closer still is
MemTX (arXiv 2607.23929, ``Transactional Belief Commit for Stateful
Agent Memory''), which argues that a memory write is not a belief
commit: writes are staged in snapshot-isolated transactions, admitted by
a validate-and-commit pipeline, and carry evidence, permissions,
provenance, and validity. Our Origin IR state layer implements exactly
this discipline in the long-form generation setting, and ShadowBench-W's
W3 is the first public benchmark that \emph{measures} whether a system
honors it. MemTX is a protocol design without a public benchmark or
evaluation data; ShadowBench-W supplies the missing measurement --- and
the measurement's first verdict (§5.3) is that transactional machinery
buys evidence, not accuracy. Any transactional-memory proposal evaluated
only against a stateless baseline should be read with that in mind.

\textbf{Document-parsing evaluation (adjacent evidence).} OmniDocBench
(arXiv 2412.07626, CVPR 2025) shows that document parsing has a public
benchmark with comprehensive annotations---and that state-writeback
evaluation does not. That gap is our position.

\textbf{Evaluation methodology.} We deliberately use deterministic rules
(with a curated vocabulary patch) rather than LLM-as-judge for W1/W3, to
avoid circularity between the generator and the judge. The vocabulary
patch (40/40 true-violation hits, 0 false positives, re-verified by
\texttt{vocab-patch-check.mjs}) is versioned as part of the benchmark.

\subsection{3. Task \& Benchmark}\label{task-benchmark}

\subsubsection{3.1 Task definition}\label{task-definition}

A long-form continuation task is a tuple \((D_0, T, S)\) where:

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \(D_0\) is the baseline corpus at one of two scales---S-level
  (chapters 1--10, ≈20K characters) or \textbf{M-level (chapters 1--50,
  ≈95K characters)}, Chinese; note the byte-level accounting in §5.4;
\item
  \(T\) is the continuation task (S: chapters 11--15; M: chapters
  51--55) with a world specification
  (\texttt{world/spec.origin/tasks/continuation.json} /
  \texttt{continuation-m.json}):
  \texttt{state\_at\_\textless{}baseline\_end\textgreater{}},
  \texttt{forbidden\_zones}, and a goal (Lin Zheng must obtain the Black
  Key from Zhao Qi);
\item
  \(S\) is the system-maintained world state.
\end{itemize}

Consistency \(C(D, S)\) holds iff every field of \(S\) is supported by
evidence in \(D\), and no statement in \(D\) contradicts \(S\).

\subsubsection{3.2 Benchmark components}\label{benchmark-components}

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{Corpus}: \texttt{corpus/ch01-10.txt} (S, ≈20K chars) and
  \texttt{corpus/ch01-50.txt} (M, ≈95K chars); UTF-8 Chinese, 3
  bytes/char.
\item
  \textbf{World spec}:
  \texttt{world/spec.origin/tasks/continuation.json} (S) and
  \texttt{continuation-m.json} (M).
\item
  \textbf{Judge W1}: CED (consistency-error density) and EPC (errors per
  100 contacts). Patched vocabulary: 40/40 hits, 25 false-positive traps
  all silent (Run \#29).
\item
  \textbf{Judge W3}: three numbers, reported separately because
  reporting one of them is selective disclosure --- \textbf{state
  accuracy} (field-level match against ground truth); \textbf{evidence
  coverage} (of the fields under test, how many carry evidence that
  points back to a source scene --- denominator is the \emph{required}
  field set); \textbf{evidence precision} (of the evidence the arm
  \emph{did} supply, how much is source-traceable --- denominator is the
  arm's own output, hence \texttt{null} when it supplies none). v0.3 of
  this paper reported precision alone and called it traceability; see
  §7.2.
\item
  \textbf{Arms}: A0 = bare model with tail truncation; A1 = cheap vector
  RAG; \textbf{A2 = prompt-only state} (told to emit and update an
  explicit world state each chapter; no validator, no evidence
  requirement, no context compiler); A3 = Origin IR state layer.
\item
  \textbf{Models}: qwen-plus, deepseek-v4-flash (both reasoning models;
  reasoning tokens accounted---see §5.4).
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{3.3 Metric protocol (honesty
boundaries)}\label{metric-protocol-honesty-boundaries}

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  W3 for stateless arms (A0/A1) is collected via one extra state-query
  round; that round's tokens count toward cost---no free lunch, and we
  say so in §5.
\item
  W1 EPC depends on the vocabulary: 13/40 hits, 27 misses before the
  patch; 40/40 after. The judge version is part of the benchmark and
  ships with it.
\item
  Judges use deterministic rules only; no LLM-as-judge (circularity
  guard).
\end{itemize}

\subsection{4. Method: The Origin IR State
Layer}\label{method-the-origin-ir-state-layer}

\begin{quote}
Read this section knowing where §5.3 ends up: on state accuracy,
everything below is matched by a prompt. We describe it in full anyway,
because it is the arm that produces evidence chains, because its failure
is a result of this paper rather than an embarrassment to be trimmed,
and because the design lessons in §4.3 were paid for and are reusable by
anyone building the arm that \emph{does} win.
\end{quote}

\subsubsection{4.1 Architecture}\label{architecture}

\begin{verbatim}
Input side: context-compiler (budget compilation)
  corpus + world spec → pruned/projected under a token budget → prompt (≈6K tokens)

Output side: commit-compiler (parse / validate / retain evidence)
  model output → parse state-change declarations → validator (deterministic rules
  + vocabulary patch) → pass: commit transaction with evidence chain
                        → fail: keep best-effort output flagged for human review
\end{verbatim}

\subsubsection{4.2 State as transactions}\label{state-as-transactions}

Every state change is committed as a transaction carrying
\texttt{valid\_from} (which event) and \texttt{evidence} (which
scene/passage). The system may reject, but it must not stay silent: a
rejected write leaves a hand-off artifact for human review (the
``docking bay'' layer).

\subsubsection{4.3 Design decisions learned from real
failures}\label{design-decisions-learned-from-real-failures}

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{ID normalization layer.} The model wrote \texttt{zhao-qi}
  instead of \texttt{char:zhao-qi}, invalidating 5 chapters. Fix:
  projections always present full IDs; the commit side normalizes
  (prefix completion, alias disambiguation). Lessons documented in the
  protocol (docs/03, empirical rule \#7).
\item
  \textbf{Judges must distinguish ``rejected'' from ``done''.} A system
  that rejects everything scores a perfect error rate of 0.0. Judgment
  requires task completion first, then quality (empirical rule \#8).
\item
  \textbf{State and text are gated separately.} Correct state does not
  imply correct text; the gate checks both (empirical rule \#9; three
  runs: errors 1/6/3 → 0/0/0).
\item
  \textbf{Vocabulary patch.} Deterministic rules have systematic synonym
  blind spots (13/40 → 40/40). The vocabulary is part of the judge and
  versioned with it.
\end{itemize}

Four findings:

\begin{enumerate}
\def\labelenumi{\arabic{enumi}.}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{The demand for state is the differentiator --- not the state
  layer.} Of A3's 43.8-point advantage over the bare model, \textbf{41.3
  points (94\%) are reproduced by A2}, which does nothing but instruct
  the model to emit and update an explicit world state. The 2.5 points
  that remain are indistinguishable from noise. Whatever the validator,
  the transaction log, and the evidence chain are worth, it is not state
  accuracy on this benchmark.
\item
  \textbf{And the cheap arm is cheaper than doing nothing.} A2 uses
  \textbf{9.7\% fewer tokens than A0}. The bare and RAG arms must
  reconstruct their state in an end-of-run probe over the full text
  (§3.3); A2 carries a compact state forward and never pays for that
  reconstruction. Higher accuracy at negative cost is not a trade-off
  --- it is a free lunch that the field has been walking past.
\item
  \textbf{RAG is the arm that actually fails.} Retrieval returns
  relevant prose and improves text (EPC 0.82), but leaves state at
  70.0\% --- below A2's 97.5\% at essentially the same token cost.
  Retrieving what was written is not the same as maintaining what is
  believed.
\item
  \textbf{The gap widens with scale.} At S-level (20K chars) A0 hit
  75.0\%; at M-level (95K chars) A0 collapses to 56.3\%. State
  corruption is a \emph{scale} defect --- which is why the demand for
  explicit state pays off more the longer the horizon.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{The method ablation (run 2026-08-13) --- and what it did to

A3 bundles three interventions: \textbf{(a) the demand} --- instructing
the model to keep an explicit, queryable state while it writes;

the machinery. It never separated (a).

models, 10 rounds each; the arm is a prompt instructing the model to
emit and update a world state per chapter --- no validator, no evidence

explaining is 94\% attributable to asking the model for something we had
never asked the baselines for. Note also that A2 \emph{outperforms A1
(RAG) by 27--36 points at lower cost}: the relevant comparison for
practitioners is not ``retrieval vs.~state layer'' but ``asking vs.~not
asking''.

than quantitative: A2 cannot produce an evidence chain at all --- not
poorly, but structurally, because nothing in a prompt-only arm
distinguishes a belief from its justification. A3 produces one for
15--27.5\% of the fields under test. A system that must answer
\emph{``why do you believe that''} still needs machinery; a system that
must only answer \emph{``what do you believe''} does not. That is a much
narrower claim than v0.3 made, and it is the one the data supports.

A3 minus validator, A3 minus evidence chain --- would tell us whether
the residual 2.5 points and the evidence capability come from the same
component. Given that 2.5 points is not significant, the honest
expectation is that the validator contributes nothing measurable here
and the evidence chain is doing all the remaining work; we have not run
it, and we do not claim it.

\subsubsection{5.5 Known limitations (self-audited, so reviewers do not
find them
first)}\label{known-limitations-self-audited-so-reviewers-do-not-find-them-first}

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{Judge/gate coupling}: A3 uses the same rules as both shield
  and yardstick; the deterministic-channel advantage carries a
  circularity component. We scope this explicitly: W3 compares only
  \emph{structured state fields} against ground truth, never free text,
  so the judge does not depend on lexical overlap with the gate's rules.
  The residual risk is that A3's internal rule list and the judge's rule
  list share a source; we report the judge fingerprints (§7) so the
  coupling is auditable, and treat W3 as an upper bound until an
  independent judge is added.
\item
  \textbf{Semantic channel not in the main score}: vocabulary patches
  cannot catch up with natural-language synonym space (Run \#23
  conclusion). W1 (EPC) therefore understates A0's true error rate if A0
  rephrases; we report the patch's 40/40 recall on the test set so the
  reader can gauge the ceiling.
\item
  \textbf{Probe protocol}: the S-level 75.0\% constant partly reflected
  a probe-template artifact (Run \#18); the M-level protocol sends
  full-text probes, and A0's M-level W3 is 56.3\% ± 23.2\% with real
  variance---the artifact is gone, not averaged over.
\item
  \textbf{Our own method does not survive its ablation on the main
  metric} (§5.3). We leave this in the limitations list rather than only
  in the results, because a reader who skims should not be able to miss
  it. The Origin IR state layer is retained in this paper as the arm
  that produces evidence chains, not as a state-accuracy method.
\item
  \textbf{The evidence result is weak in absolute terms}: 15--27.5\%
  coverage is a capability demonstration, not a solved problem. We do
  not know whether the shortfall is a model limitation, a prompt-design
  limitation, or a defect in our commit-compiler; we have not run the
  experiment that would tell us.
\item
  \textbf{(b) and (c) remain entangled}: validator vs.~evidence chain
  (§5.3, final paragraph).
\item
  \textbf{Single team, three models, one corpus}: the full four-arm
  design ran on two models; a third (frontier) model was run on the bare
  arm only (§5.2.1). Single corpus, Chinese fiction. Declared bounds,
  not claims.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{7.1 Who validates the
validator?}\label{who-validates-the-validator}

A self-test that kills no mutant is indistinguishable from no self-test:
it keeps passing after every refactor while the benchmark quietly hands
out points to every arm. We therefore mutate the \emph{judges}
themselves --- plausible human errors injected into \texttt{ced.mjs}
(W1), \texttt{detect-score.mjs} (W2) and \texttt{state-diff.mjs} (W3),
one at a time, each followed by a full self-test run and a revert.

Mutants include: W3 accuracy pinned to full marks, W3 strict comparison
degraded to string comparison, W3 evidence checks loosened, evidence
coverage reverted to a self-selected denominator, W1 error count pinned
to zero, W1 chain-of-custody check for the Black Key removed, and W2
recall computed over \emph{found} rather than \emph{planted} errors.

\textbf{Result: 12 injected, 12 killed, 0 survived}
(\texttt{eval/mutation-check.mjs\ -\/-json}). The runner also reports
\emph{stale} mutants --- anchors that no longer match the source ---
because a mutation that can never fire would otherwise masquerade as
coverage; this fired once during the 2026-08-13 judge change and was
repaired rather than ignored.

This does not remove the judge/gate coupling declared in §5.5. And §7.2
shows what it does \textbf{not} catch.

\subsubsection{7.2 What mutation testing cannot catch: a metric that was
wrong by
definition}\label{what-mutation-testing-cannot-catch-a-metric-that-was-wrong-by-definition}

Version 0.3 of this paper reported \textbf{``100\% evidence
traceability''} for the state layer. The number was computed correctly,
was under assertion, survived every mutant, and was wrong to report.

\texttt{evidenceTraceability} divided source-traceable evidence entries
by \emph{the entries the arm chose to supply}. An arm supplying evidence
for four of eight required fields, all four well-formed, scored 100\%.
One of our ten qwen rounds is exactly that case. Arms supplying no
evidence scored \texttt{null} --- excluded from means rather than
counted as zero. Measured against the fields the benchmark actually asks
about, the state layer's coverage is \textbf{27.5\% (qwen) / 15.0\%
(deepseek)}.

Both numbers are true; they answer different questions. We were
reporting a \textbf{precision} and reading it as a \textbf{coverage}.
The judge now emits both, \texttt{evidenceCoverage} counts a field only
when the evidence key matches the tested \texttt{id.field} exactly
(evidence for \texttt{char:lin-zheng.location} does not discharge
\texttt{char:lin-zheng.knows}), and arms with no evidence score 0 rather
than \texttt{null}.

The methodological point generalizes beyond this paper. Mutation testing
asks \emph{``does the code still do what the code intends?''} --- it
cannot ask \emph{``is that intention the right one?''} Our 9/9 kill rate
was real and told us nothing about this defect, because no mutation of a
metric's implementation can reveal that the metric's \textbf{definition}
flatters its author. The check that caught it was recomputing a
published number from raw data with a denominator chosen by someone
looking for the flattering assumption --- which, in this case, was the
same authors, one day later, and is the only reason it is in this paper
instead of in a reviewer's report.
